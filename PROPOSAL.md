# Fair value of the Rand: a critical look at the current model, and where to take it next

## What the app currently computes

The app estimates a "fair value" for ZAR against a handful of currencies using relative purchasing-power parity (PPP): the idea that if South African inflation runs hotter than a trading partner's, the Rand needs to weaken by roughly that differential over time just to keep buying power even between the two countries. Rather than picking one arbitrary historical date as the PPP anchor -- which is the classic weakness of simple PPP calculators, since the answer you get is extremely sensitive to which base year you happen to choose -- the app takes every historical date as a candidate anchor, projects each one forward to today using the realised inflation differential, and fits a smoothed trend through the resulting ensemble of estimates. That trend is the headline "fair value," and the gap between it and the actual rate at any point in time is read as a rough over/undervaluation signal. This is a genuine improvement on single-anchor PPP, and it's worth being clear-eyed about what it does and doesn't fix.

## Where relative PPP falls short as a fair-value concept

Inflation-differential PPP is, at bottom, a statement about *relative price levels*, not about the exchange rate itself. It implicitly assumes that the only thing that should move a currency over the long run is the accumulated gap in consumer price inflation between two countries. Four things complicate that assumption enough to matter for a currency like the Rand.

The first is the Balassa-Samuelson effect: as an economy's tradable-sector productivity rises relative to a richer trading partner's, its real exchange rate tends to appreciate even with identical inflation, because non-tradable prices (services, rent, labour) catch up to tradable-sector wage growth. South Africa's productivity growth relative to the US, UK, Switzerland, Singapore and China has not been uniform across decades, so a model that only tracks headline CPI differentials is silently absorbing (or missing) this effect rather than accounting for it.

The second is that CPI baskets are not directly comparable. South Africa's basket weights are different from Switzerland's or Singapore's -- different housing treatment, different weights on food and energy, different survey methodologies. Relative PPP assumes away these differences; in reality a chunk of the "differential inflation" the model measures may reflect basket composition rather than genuine relative price-level drift.

The third, and probably the most important for the Rand specifically, is that South Africa is a commodity exporter with a currency that is unusually sensitive to terms of trade and global risk appetite. Gold, platinum-group metals and (to a lesser extent) broader commodity prices explain a meaningful share of ZAR's medium-run swings, entirely independent of the inflation differential -- the 2008-2011 commodity boom and the 2014-2016 commodity bust both moved the Rand by more, and faster, than any plausible inflation-differential story would predict. A model that never looks at commodity prices or the current account is blind to a large part of what actually drives this particular currency.

The fourth is capital flows and risk premia. Emerging-market currencies carry a risk premium that expands and contracts with global risk sentiment (US rate cycles, EM contagion episodes, South Africa-specific political and fiscal risk -- state capture, load-shedding, ratings downgrades). None of that shows up in a CPI series. PPP is, at best, a *very* long-run anchor (multi-decade); on any horizon shorter than that, capital flows dominate.

The app's ensemble-and-trend approach helps with the base-period problem specifically, but it doesn't address any of these four issues -- it's still, fundamentally, a single-factor model built entirely on inflation prints.

## Complementary and alternative approaches worth considering

**Uncovered interest rate parity (UIP)** offers a genuinely different, complementary signal: the interest rate differential between South African and foreign government bonds is, in theory, the market's own implied expectation of currency depreciation. It's cheap to compute (just need SA and foreign yield curves, also available from FRED), and because it's forward-looking and market-priced, it tends to be more responsive on shorter horizons than inflation-differential PPP, even though UIP itself is known to fail systematically in the data (the "forward premium puzzle"). Showing PPP's long-run drift alongside UIP's short-run market-implied drift on the same chart would give a genuinely more complete picture than either alone.

**A terms-of-trade or commodity-price overlay** is probably the highest-value, lowest-effort addition given how commodity-sensitive the Rand is. Even without building a full regression model, plotting a commodity price index (or South Africa's terms-of-trade series, also on FRED) alongside the existing deviation-from-trend chart would let you visually sanity-check how much of any given "overvaluation" or "undervaluation" reading is actually just tracking commodity prices rather than a genuine PPP misalignment.

**A BEER (behavioural equilibrium exchange rate) model** is the standard step up in sophistication used by central banks and the IMF: a multivariate regression of the real exchange rate on a small set of fundamentals -- terms of trade, net foreign asset position, relative productivity, government consumption, interest rate differential, trade openness. It's more work (needs several additional FRED/World Bank series and a regression specification), but it directly addresses the "PPP is a single-factor model" critique above, and would sit naturally as a second "fair value" line on the same chart for direct comparison against the PPP estimate.

**REER band benchmarking** is a lighter-weight alternative to BEER: instead of a structural fair-value level, compare South Africa's real effective (trade-weighted) exchange rate to its own long-run historical mean or median, with a band at (say) one standard deviation. This sidesteps having to specify *why* the rate should sit where it does, and just asks whether it's unusually far from its own history -- a genuinely different and often more robust framing than any point-estimate model.

**Uncertainty quantification from the existing ensemble** is close to free, since the app already computes an entire distribution of anchor-implied fair values, one per historical date -- it currently only shows the trend through them. Plotting the 10th-90th percentile spread of that ensemble as a shaded band around the trend line would convey, honestly, how much the "fair value" number actually depends on which slice of history you trust, rather than presenting a single trend line as more precise than it really is.

## A practical starting point

If the goal is to make the app noticeably better without a large rebuild, the ensemble uncertainty band is worth doing first: the underlying numbers already exist in `fair_value_analysis()`, so it's a chart change, not a new data pipeline, and it directly addresses the single biggest thing this kind of model overstates -- its own precision. A commodity-price or terms-of-trade overlay is the next most valuable addition given how much of ZAR's actual behaviour it would help explain, and it only requires pulling one more FRED series into `R/01_data.R`. A UIP-based short-run signal and a full BEER regression are worthwhile medium-term projects if this app is going to be used for anything beyond a long-run sanity check, but they're a meaningfully bigger undertaking than the two additions above and would be better scoped as their own piece of work once the core rebuild has been used for a while.
